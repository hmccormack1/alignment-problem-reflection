The Alignment Problem, and a toy version of one of its stories

Brian Christian, The Alignment Problem (2020) · Hadley McCormack · Fall 2026

1. My reflection on the book

I won't sugar coat it. Overall, I found this book pretty dry. It's somewhat long and packed with researcher names and lab histories. I will say that I think one reason I found it so dry was because I read it alongside this course, and a lot of the concepts were things we covered in class (in some cases multiple classes). Bias, interpretability, reward design, and the basics of reinforcement learning (which we just covered this week) are a few examples of this. I will say that I also liked how well the book mapped onto the class since it allowed me to dive deeper into the concepts and see more examples and details that we didn't cover. At the same time, it's probably also why parts of it felt redundant. Funny enough, the book's introduction on how neural networks and the field in general came about (like the story of the perceptron) is something we talked about in AIPI 520.

I felt like the book dwelled a lot on the backstory of AI and the evolution of it in the beginning, which was a core reason why I struggled to get into it. I feel like when I chose the book based on the title, I was expecting it to be focused on aligning AI with human values, but in reality this felt like a smaller portion of the book (really only focusing on this in the last third of the book if I remember correctly). Nevertheless, I do think it is an important foundation to hear about the history, and it went by faster once I switched to the audiobook. I did think it was cool to see how each idea arrived usually as a fix for the previous idea's problem. I felt like this was useful foundation information, and it also made the field feel like an actual story instead of a list of topics.

My favorite part of the book was the illustrative examples that the author thoughtfully picked out. "Prophecy" was my favorite section for this reason. The COMPAS case, where a risk score was accurate by one definition of fairness and unfair by another, and the image-recognition example, where a system labeled a Black man as a monkey, made "aligned with us" more concrete. The examples showed weren't hypothetical future risks. They were systems doing exactly what they were built to do while harming real people, and that's what I'll remember from the book.

My last praise for the book is that I truly felt like it broke down the technical concepts of machine learning and artificial intelligence in a very digestible way. Because of this, even though I had learned a lot of the content before in our classes, it made me look at some topics, such as how the first artificial neuron came about, in a different way, at times even a way that allowed me to understand it better.

One aspect of the book where I was kind of skeptical was the optimism of the later sections. To me, the fairness chapter shows that no amount of math settles which definition of fairness we should want and that people have to choose. However, the chapters on inferring values and handling uncertainty lean on technical approaches to learn "what we want," which seems to assume there's a coherent "we" to learn from. I think the book does a good job of being honest about this, but I still think it reads as more confident that research will close the gap than I am. Deployment incentives and who gets to decide felt underplayed next to the research stories.

So overall, although it was a slow read for me, I think it was a valuable book for me to read, and it helped me build on AI foundational topics from class but also taught me new examples and new applications that I otherwise never would have thought about. I recommended the book to my dad!

2. A toy version of the story that clicked for me

The Transparency chapter tells the story of a hospital model that learned patients with asthma were less likely to die of pneumonia. The reason wasn't that asthma is protective. It was that those patients were sent straight to intensive care, which saved them, so the pattern in the data was actually the effect of a treatment the model couldn't see. I picked this one because this was an example that I remembered (so it clearly stuck with me) but also because it's a version of the book's point I could actually rebuild and poke at. Step 3: Three ways to respond

A readable model lets a person notice the odd asthma bar and act on it. Here are three responses that are compared in the next step: (a) keep the model as trained, (b) delete the asthma term by hand, or (c) retrain with "got ICU" as a feature and then predict assuming no automatic ICU. Deleting the term is a blunt fix because it also throws away asthma's real risk. Retraining with the hidden treatment included is the better one, and it needs someone to know the treatment exists.

Step 4: Deploy it, then change the policy

Now the hospital reads the model's low score for asthma patients as "safe," stops the automatic ICU rule and sends low-risk patients home. In deployment, nobody gets the automatic ICU, so every asthma patient carries their true, higher risk. 3. Where I've seen this apply to my own projects

F1 pit-stop model. For Project 1 of this class, I compared XGBoost explained with SHAP against an Explainable Boosting Machine. In a way, that is the book's Transparency debate in miniature: explain a black box after the fact or use a model you can read directly. EBMs are descendants of the additive models behind the asthma story, so seeing the book's example and my own project line up was something I found to be satisfying. The Imitation chapter also applies to this specific project. A model that predicts when teams pit learns what strategists did rather than what was best, so any mistakes or habits in the history become part of what it treats as normal. This was something that was a listed limitation of my project, so I appreciated how this lined up with what the book talked about.

TikTok stock model. This project is still in the works, and we are just now thinking about modeling, but what I have learned from the book will serve as a useful checklist for us. The Representation chapter says to ask whose data you're learning from, which relates because TikTok users aren't a neutral sample of investors. In addition, "predict stock movement" (which is what we are using Tik Toks to do) is a proxy, so it's worth asking what we actually want the model to be good at before we choose a number to optimize.

4. What changed in my thinking

Before this book, and especially before this class, I thought of alignment mostly as a future, advanced-AI problem. The book's examples show it's already a data and objectives problem in ordinary systems, and the toy demo above shows how little it takes. A model can fit its data perfectly and still be wrong about the world. I also learned more than I expected from material I thought I already knew: the story of how the first artificial neuron came about made more sense to me the second time, and seeing each idea arrive as a fix for the previous one made the field feel connected instead of like a list of topics. One thing I genuinely hadn't known before the book was that neuroscientists found dopamine neurons seem to signal prediction errors, which is the same idea behind the temporal-difference learning we covered in class. Seeing that the algorithm came from studying brains made reinforcement learning feel less like an abstract trick. To generalize this, the book showed me how much AI borrows from other fields, like psychology and neuroscience, which I hadn't really thought about before.

The habit I'm taking from it is to ask what the data doesn't show, even before asking how accurate the model is. One thing I'm still unsure about after finishing the book is how much of the problem technical fixes can solve, since the fairness chapter suggests parts of it are really about values.
